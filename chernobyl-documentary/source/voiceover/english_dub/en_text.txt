April twenty-sixth, nineteen eighty-six. | Pripyat, Soviet Ukraine.
Nearby, at the Chernobyl nuclear plant, | a test was underway.
One twenty-three a.m.
The reactor had exploded. | Its core burned under the open sky.
Firefighters arrived within minutes. | They had no idea what they were fighting.
No protective suits. No radiation warnings. | Just the fire in front of them.
Many of them died | within weeks.
And Pripyat | was still fast asleep.
This Ferris wheel was due to open on the first of May. | It never turned.
Morning came. | A column of smoke rose on the horizon.
But nobody was told anything.
It was Saturday. | Children went to school, couples got married. | Life went on as usual.
Meanwhile, the reactor burned. | Helicopters began dropping sand and lead into it.
Thirty-six hours later, | loudspeakers made the announcement. | The whole city must leave.
Outside the city, | a long line of buses was waiting.
People were told | to bring only essentials and documents.
Fifty thousand people. | Three and a half hours.
They thought | it would only be a few days.
By that evening, | Pripyat was completely empty.
For the next two weeks, | helicopters kept dropping sand, lead and boron into the reactor. | Five thousand tonnes.
The machines that fought this battle | became so radioactive | they had to be abandoned.
In September, debris had to be cleared from the roof of Unit Three. | Machines could not work there.
So men were sent instead. | Each man had only ninety seconds.
By November, | a concrete tomb stood over the ruined reactor. | The Sarcophagus.
Around six hundred thousand people | helped contain this disaster. | They were called the liquidators.
And Pripyat | has stood empty ever since.
The world | still had not been told.
On the night of April twenty-eighth, | Soviet television gave the news just twenty seconds.
On the first of May, in Kyiv, | the May Day parade marched on as usual.
People did not know | there was radiation in the air.
Water had flooded the chambers beneath the reactor. | If the molten core reached it, | a second explosion could follow.
Three men went down into the dark water | and opened the valves.
One hundred and sixteen thousand people | were evacuated from the thirty-kilometre zone.
On May tenth, | the fire in the core finally went out.
But by then, | the radioactive cloud had spread across Europe.
The pine forest beside the plant | turned red from the radiation, and died.
It was bulldozed | and buried in the ground.
In Pripyat's schools, | books still lie open on the desks.
In October nineteen eighty-six, | right next to the ruined reactor, | Unit One was restarted.
In July nineteen eighty-seven, there was a trial. | Three plant officials | were sentenced to ten years.
The lights of Pripyat | never came on again.
Some elderly people ignored the government. | They returned to their villages.
For them, | this land was home.
In the nineties, | thyroid cancer spread among those | who had been children in eighty-six.
Radioactive iodine | had reached their bodies through milk.
October eleventh, nineteen ninety-one. | A fire broke out in the turbine hall of Unit Two.
That unit | never ran again.
In December, the Soviet Union collapsed. | Now the zone was the responsibility | of an independent Ukraine.
Checkpoints were built around the zone. | Two thousand six hundred square kilometres, | sealed off.
Only people with permits | could enter.
In the nineties, | looters stripped Pripyat almost bare.
Furniture, metal, even radiators. | Much of it was radioactive.
And slowly, | the forest began to take the city back.
The same road, the same bicycle. | Now among the trees.
Hidden in the forest was a secret Soviet radar. | Duga. | Now it too stands silent.
The hastily built Sarcophagus | was starting to crumble.
Its walls were cracked, | and rainwater leaked in through the roof.
One by one, | the other reactors were shut down. | In ninety-six, Unit One.
December fifteenth, two thousand. | One seventeen in the afternoon.
The last reactor, Unit Three, | was shut down for good.
After twenty-three years, | the Chernobyl plant fell silent.
Where people left, | nature returned.
In nineteen ninety-eight, rare wild horses were released here. | Their herd is still growing today.
Wolves, elk, deer, | wild boar.
Without humans, | their numbers grew higher than in the forests around.
Pripyat was slowly | disappearing into the forest.
The radiation is still here. | But the absence of people | was a far greater relief for the animals.
The old Sarcophagus | could not last much longer.
Many countries came together | to build a giant steel arch beside it.
Two hundred and fifty-seven metres wide. | One hundred and eight metres tall.
The largest structure | ever moved on land.
In November twenty sixteen, | it was slid on rails | over the old reactor.
This arch will protect the world | from this reactor | for a hundred years.
But the price | was paid by people.
Firefighters, operators, liquidators. | The ones who saved the world.
In the first few weeks, thirty-one people died. | They were buried in Moscow, | in sealed coffins.
About three hundred and fifty thousand people | lost their homes forever. | Hundreds of villages vanished from the map.
How many lives were lost in total | is still debated.
From four thousand | to more than sixty thousand.
In twenty eleven, | the zone was opened to tourists.
People come to see the Ferris wheel | that never turned.
The Azure swimming pool. | For ten years after the disaster, | liquidators still swam here.
Beneath the reactor, | a mass of melted fuel still remains. | The Elephant's Foot.
Once, standing near it for just a few minutes | could kill you.
In twenty nineteen, | one hundred and twenty-four thousand people came here.
Today, | a giant steel arch stands over Chernobyl.
Beneath it lies the reactor | that changed the world.
Pripyat. | Once home to forty-nine thousand people.
Today, there is only forest here. | And silence.
Parts of this land | will stay radioactive for thousands of years.
This story is in memory of all | who paid the price.

But how did it happen? | That night, at Unit Four, | a safety test was running.
The question was whether the turbine | could keep the pumps running | for a while after a power cut.
The test was delayed. | For hours, the reactor ran at very low power.
In that state, | this reactor became dangerously unstable.
At one twenty-three a.m., | the test began.
Seconds later, | the emergency button, A Z five, | was pressed.
But a design flaw meant the power, | instead of falling, | surged many times over.
And within seconds, | the reactor exploded.

On the morning of April twenty-eighth, | alarms went off at a nuclear plant in Sweden.
Radioactive dust | was found on the workers' clothes.
Tests showed | the radiation did not come from their plant.
The wind was blowing from the east. | From the Soviet Union.
A radioactive cloud | had spread over several European countries.
The world was asking questions. | But the Soviet Union stayed silent.
Finally, that night, | a twenty-second report aired on television.
An accident has occurred. | That was all.

Chunks of graphite | lay scattered across the reactor roofs.
The radiation there was so intense | that even machines stopped working.
Robots were brought in from abroad. | They too broke down within minutes.
In the end, | the job had to be done by humans.
They were called | bio-robots.
Each man could stay on the roof | for just ninety seconds.
Shovel a few pieces over the edge, | and get back.
By November, | a concrete structure stood over the reactor.

The firefighters | were flown to Moscow.
Hospital Number Six. | A special hospital for radiation patients.
The doctors tried everything. | Even bone marrow transplants.
But the radiation doses | were far too high.
One by one, | they began to die.
They were buried in zinc coffins | at Mitino Cemetery.
Because even their bodies | were giving off radiation.
They paid for saving others | with their own lives.

Pripyat was founded in nineteen seventy | for the plant's workers.
It was a young city. | The average age was just twenty-six.
It had schools, a hospital, a cinema, | and a swimming pool.
People left for a few days. | Their belongings stayed behind.
Later, homes were looted. | Everything was scattered.
In the classrooms, | books still lie on the desks.
A city of fifty thousand | emptied in a single day.
And it never filled again.

After Chernobyl, | the world's nuclear rules changed.
In other reactors like it, | the design flaws were fixed.
Today, | many of them have been shut down.
In twenty twenty-two, | the zone was back in the news.
During the war, soldiers dug trenches | in the soil of the Red Forest.
Five weeks later, | they pulled out.
Chernobyl | is not just an accident.
It is a reminder | that the price of one mistake | is paid by generations.
